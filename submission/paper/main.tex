\begin{abstract}
Language can push a listener toward an action, narrow the available choices,
or do both. This paper presents \LNGF{}, a benchmark for measuring this
negative agentive force in de-identified multi-turn dialogues. It contains
4{,}066 dialogues in a 3{,}432-dialogue training split and a disjoint
634-dialogue held-out split. Each dialogue is represented with three linked
components:
a taxonomy of four families and fifteen types, seven measurement dimensions,
and linguistic cue guidelines for interpreting each score. The benchmark evaluates label consistency,
agreement between two independent annotators, three prediction tasks, a
two-stage dimension-aware pipeline, and transfer to MentalManip, MultiManip,
TalkDown, and ToxicChat.
\end{abstract}


\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Agentive force, computational social science, psycholinguistic framework,
large language models, manipulation detection.
\end{IEEEkeywords}
\section{Introduction}
Language can change what a listener sees as possible or acceptable. A request,
threat, moral appeal, or deceptive statement can make one action easier to
choose and other actions harder to choose. This listener-directed effect is
called \emph{\afd{}}. The benchmark focuses on its negative forms: language that
pressures, constrains, or steers a listener against a neutral choice. Ordinary
requests, advice, and rational persuasion are included as controls.

Existing NLP resources cover parts of this problem. Toxicity and
condescension datasets focus on explicit hostility~\cite{toxicchat2023,
talkdown2023}; persuasion datasets often target news text and rhetorical
framing~\cite{piskorski2023}; and dialogue datasets on psychological
manipulation focus on narrower covert strategies such as gaslighting and
intimidation~\cite{mentalmanip,multimanip,reament}. Compliance-gaining
research offers useful strategy taxonomies~\cite{marwell1967,wiseman1981,
cialdini1984}, but these resources use different label spaces. The central
question is whether different negative language strategies can be represented
and measured in one space. Transfer to strategies outside the annotation is a
downstream test of that representation.



To address this gap, \LNGF{} defines a shared representation and tests whether
it is useful for prediction and transfer. Its contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A unified three-layer framework.} The framework combines a
four-family, fifteen-type strategy taxonomy with seven measurement dimensions
and cue guidelines. The dimensions separate the force of a push, its
effect on the listener's options, its pressure channel, and its directness.
\item \textbf{A new annotated benchmark.} The release contains 4{,}066
dialogues with dialogue-level intensity and seven-dimension annotations,
organized into a training split and a disjoint held-out split.
The benchmark relates dimension scores to binary pressure and intensity labels.
\item \textbf{An experimental protocol.} The benchmark defines detection,
strategy recognition, and intensity prediction tasks. It also provides a
two-stage dimension-aware pipeline, a direct zero-shot baseline, and tests of
dimension structure and transfer to unseen types.
\end{enumerate}
\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Datasets of Manipulation and Persuasion}
Compliance-gaining research provides the conceptual starting point. Marwell
and Schmitt~\cite{marwell1967} and Wiseman and Schenck-Hamlin~
\cite{wiseman1981} proposed strategy inventories, and Cialdini~
\cite{cialdini1984} organized common influence principles such as reciprocity,
social proof, and authority. In NLP, SemEval-2023 Task~3 detects persuasion
techniques in online news~\cite{piskorski2023}. MentalManip, MultiManip~
\cite{mentalmanip,multimanip}, and ReaMent~\cite{reament} study covert
manipulation in dialogue. These resources use different units and label
spaces. Many omit ordinary non-coercive controls such as requests, advice, and rational
persuasion. Toxicity~\cite{toxicchat2023}, condescension~\cite{talkdown2023},
and microaggression datasets~\cite{breitfeller2019} cover hostile language,
but hostility is only one possible pressure channel.

\LNGF{} addresses this gap by putting ordinary non-coercive controls and negative,
listener-restricting strategies in one dialogue-level measurement space. It
does not replace these resources; it provides a common representation for
comparing the pressure they study.

\subsection{Speech Acts and Pressure}
Speech-act theory studies how people use language to perform actions such as
requesting, ordering, and promising~\cite{austin1962,searle1969}.
This perspective helps explain why aggressive words alone cannot identify
pressure. For example, ``A good daughter would stay'' contains no insult,
but invokes a family obligation to pressure the listener to stay.
The analysis identifies the action requested by the speaker and the ways the
dialogue makes refusal harder. The annotation records pressure expressed in
the dialogue, not whether the listener actually complies.

\subsection{Normative Influence}
Normative influence encourages compliance by appealing to duties, social
roles, moral expectations, or authority. For example, ``You are the team
leader; it is your duty'' uses a role obligation to pressure the listener.
Research on moral foundations provides background for understanding moral
and authority-based appeals~\cite{graham2009}. These forms of influence
motivate the normative-pressure dimension in our framework.

\section{Framework: A Unified Account of \afd}
\label{sec:framework}
Negative agentive force is represented at three levels. The strategy layer
describes what the speaker does. The measurement layer records how much
pressure is applied. The cue layer links each judgment to words and discourse
patterns that can support it.
\subsection{Definition and Inclusion Criterion}
The \emph{\afd{}} of an utterance or dialogue is the pressure it places on a
listener's behavior or decision space. It can push the listener toward the
speaker's preferred action or make other actions harder to choose. There are
three properties of agentive force. First, the pressure does not need to
succeed; it can be present even when the listener resists. Second, one
utterance can use more than one channel, such as guilt and social proof.
Third, pressure has a degree, so strategies can differ in strength as well as
type.

A discourse has \emph{\afd{}} when it targets a recognizable listener action
or decision and tries to drive or restrict that behavior. The pressure may
come from a request, inducement, norm, emotion, deception, or hostility. The
taxonomy provides labels for the cases covered in this dataset, but it is not
intended to cover every possible strategy. Pure information exchange and
emotional venting without a behavioral target are negative examples.

\subsection{Three-Layer Architecture}
Layer~1 describes the speaker's tactic with fifteen types in four families.
Layer~2 records the amount and source of pressure along seven dimensions.
Layer~3 provides guidelines for connecting scores to observable words,
constructions, and discourse patterns. For example, an ultimatum can receive a high option-constraint
score because it removes alternatives, while an indirect moral appeal can
receive a high normative-pressure score even without an insult. The three
layers make different strategies comparable and provide evidence for error
analysis.

\subsection{Strategy Taxonomy}
\label{sec:taxonomy}
Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} presents the taxonomy. The four families organize
the current negative-force scope by the dominant mechanism. Family A uses
open requests, advice, orders, and reasoning. Family B motivates through an
expected benefit or harm. Family C is social-normative: it asks the listener
to act because of a duty, role, moral rule, authority, past favor, or group
norm. Family D uses less direct tactics, including emotional exploitation,
deception, false dilemmas, and hostility. These families are
descriptive organizing units, not mutually exclusive psychological causes,
and one dialogue may receive multiple type labels. The final column in
Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} records the dominant mechanism used by each type;
these mechanisms are descriptive and do not imply mutually exclusive causes.

\subsection{Measurement Dimensions}
\label{sec:dims}
Table~\ref{tab:dims} defines seven scores on a continuous 0--1 scale and gives
each score a four-level label. Scores are assigned at both turn and dialogue
level. The scores describe different parts of the same pressure. They are a
measurement design for this benchmark, not a complete list of psychological
traits. Their value depends on agreement, discrimination, and transfer.

The dimensions answer different questions. $D_1$ measures how strongly the
speaker pushes for an action. $D_2$ measures how much the listener's choice
space is narrowed. A direct request can have high $D_1$ but low $D_2$ if the
listener still has several clear alternatives. An ultimatum can have high
$D_2$ because it presents few acceptable alternatives. $D_3$, $D_4$, and
$D_6$ identify different pressure channels: social norms, emotional leverage,
and overt hostility. A message may use one channel without using the others.

$D_5$ measures factual or memory distortion. For example, ``I never said
that; you misremember'' can be deceptive even when it is polite. An insult
can be hostile without being deceptive, so $D_5$ and $D_6$ are separate.
$D_7$ measures how directly the pressure is expressed. ``You must stay'' is
more explicit than ``A good daughter would stay'', but explicitness alone does
not determine pressure strength. Together, the seven scores describe the push,
the effect on available choices, the pressure channel, and the form of the
expression.

The data also support simple checks of the dimension design. A moral-pressure
dialogue should usually have high $D_3$, while a polite indirect appeal may
have lower $D_7$. Family-level profiles and clustering test whether different
strategy families concentrate on different dimensions.

Two short examples show why context matters. In a normal request, one speaker
says, ``Could you close the window?'' and the other replies, ``Sure.'' This is
an A1 request with little pressure. The same request can carry more pressure
when it follows ``I helped you last time,'' because the added context invokes
C6 reciprocity. The wording alone is almost unchanged, but the dialogue-level
profile changes because the listener's decision is framed as a debt.


\subsection{Dimension Structure and Interdependence}
The dimensions are not assumed independent; their correlation structure is
an empirical object with explicit, falsifiable expectations.
(i)~$D_1$ should correlate with $D_2$ (the harder the push, the narrower the
options), with transparent A1 as the predicted exception. (ii)~$D_3$ and
$D_4$ should co-occur in family~C (guilt and moral appeal are emotionally
loaded) but separate in family~B (threats are affectively cold).
(iii)~$D_5$ should be roughly orthogonal to the others---gaslighting occurs
in both polite and hostile registers---so a strong correlation with $D_6$ or
$D_7$ would indicate annotation bias. (iv)~$D_7$ should correlate negatively
with $D_5$: covert strategies are typically indirect. The statistical
analysis (Section~\ref{sec:dataset}) tests these via correlation matrices
and per-family profiles; deviations are reported and used to revise the
guideline rather than discarded.


\section{Dataset Construction and Analysis}
\label{sec:dataset}
\subsection{Design Principles}
The dataset must cover the intended negative-force scope, provide comparable
labels for every dialogue, and include hard negatives that separate pressure
from mere toxicity or ordinary requests.
Four principles guide construction. (i)~\emph{De-identified multi-turn
dialogues}: conversational interaction with the turn structure and pragmatic
context needed for dialogue-level analysis.
(ii)~\emph{Scope coverage}: negative-force and non-force dialogue across
the 0--5 range, so benign controls prevent degenerate models that classify
any request as manipulation. (iii)~\emph{Comparable measurement}: every
instance receives the same seven-dimension annotation on one scale.
(iv)~\emph{Hard negatives}: polite coercion, sarcastic requests, and
superficially kind manipulation stress-test lexical shortcuts.

\subsection{Sources and Sampling}
The release contains 4{,}066 de-identified, everyday multi-turn dialogues.
We use 3{,}432 dialogues as the training split and 634 disjoint dialogues as
the held-out split (Section~\ref{sec:firstrelease}). Each dialogue has binary
pressure and 0--5 intensity annotations, which are used to evaluate the
seven-dimension profiles. The release contributes new annotations, a unified
taxonomy, and an evaluation protocol. The dialogues are de-identified, and
provenance and licensing information are documented with the release.

\subsection{Annotation Protocol and Negative Cases}
The annotation follows the decision tree in Figure~\ref{fig:tree}. First,
the annotator checks whether the dialogue targets a listener action or
decision. If it does not, the dialogue is a negative example. Second, the
annotator assigns all applicable strategy types. For example, one dialogue may
contain both a moral appeal and social proof. Third, the annotator assigns the
0--5 intensity and the seven dimension scores. Each dimension receives a
continuous 0--1 score and a four-level label: None, Low, Moderate, or High.

Negative examples use the same dimension scale as positive examples. Typical
negative examples include pure information exchange, emotional venting with
no behavioral target, and information-seeking questions. Advice that also
acts as a subtle request receives every applicable type, while the dimension
scores describe its dominant pressure channel. The released annotations are
produced by an instruction-tuned LLM at temperature 0 using this protocol.

\subsection{Quality Control and Consistency Validation}
\label{sec:qc}
Quality control combines three checks: agreement between independent annotators,
consistency with the binary pressure and intensity labels, and the expected
monotonicity of predicted intensity. For each dimension, the analysis reports Spearman
correlations with the binary and 0--5 labels and its AUC for separating
coercive from non-coercive dialogues (Table~\ref{tab:full}). It also checks
whether predicted intensity increases with the gold label and whether the
inter-dimension correlation matrix contains pathological coupling
(Figures~\ref{fig:mono-full} and~\ref{fig:dim-corr-full}). These checks test
whether the dimensions provide reproducible structure beyond a single binary
judgment.

Two graduate students in Computer Technology, including the first author,
independently annotated 150 dialogues using a shared annotation rubric.
Both annotators were proficient in reading English dialogues. Neither
annotator had access to the model outputs during annotation.
The sample was drawn from the 3{,}432-dialogue training split by randomly selecting
75 dialogues from each binary-label class without replacement, followed by
shuffling (random seed 2026). This balanced design covers both classes;
the reported agreement therefore describes this sample rather than an
unweighted estimate for the full dataset.
Table~\ref{tab:iaa}
reports inter-annotator agreement on the discrete labels. Intensity reaches
substantial agreement (quadratic-weighted $\kappa{=}0.823$), as do normative
pressure (0.830), toxicity (0.820), option constraint (0.776), directive
force (0.771), and emotional pressure (0.758). Explicitness reaches moderate
agreement (0.543). These estimates concern discrete labels and do not directly
establish the reliability of the continuous model scores.

\subsection{Statistical Analyses}
This analysis tests whether the seven dimensions capture distinct parts of
pressure. It compares each score with the binary and 0--5 labels, inspects
inter-dimension correlations, compares profiles for coercive and non-coercive
dialogues, and tests whether predicted intensity follows the ordered labels.
Type-level results are reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}.


\subsection{Consistency Check on the Held-Out Set}
\label{sec:firstrelease}
The held-out analysis asks whether the seven-dimensional profile follows the
pressure and intensity labels before the full set is analyzed. Agreement is a
necessary check, but it does not show that the dimensions cause changes in
listener behavior.
The held-out split contains 634 dialogues annotated under
the unified seven-dimension scheme. It contains 332 coercive (52.4\%) and 302 non-coercive
dialogues, and gold intensity is distributed as
170/54/78/64/107/161 over levels 0--5, so hard negatives and the
high-intensity tail are both represented.

\section{Experimental Design and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:exp}
The experiments test whether the agentive-force profile supports prediction
beyond description. T1 measures pressure detection, T2 strategy recognition,
and T3 intensity prediction. Ablation and cross-domain tests measure the role
of individual dimensions and transfer to other resources.
\subsection{Tasks}
\LNGF{} defines three tasks at increasing granularity.
\textbf{T1 (agentive-force detection)} is a binary classification of whether a
dialogue exerts negative pressure beyond ordinary non-coercive controls.
\textbf{T2 (type recognition)} is a fifteen-way multi-label classification
of the strategies in Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy}; type labels are released for
both the held-out split and the training split. \textbf{T3 (intensity
prediction)} is ordinal regression of the 0--5 intensity, evaluated with
quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) and accuracy-within-$\pm1$.

\subsection{Two-Stage Pipeline}
The two-stage pipeline tests whether the explicit profile adds useful
structure beyond a direct judgment. An upstream LLM converts each dialogue
into turn-level and dialogue-level dimension scores, and a downstream model
uses them for T1--T3. For comparison, a direct judge predicts the label from
the dialogue without producing the seven-dimension profile. The experiments compare
three ways to combine information: turn-only, dialogue-only, and turn-plus-
dialogue representations.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
All parsers run at temperature~$0$. T1 reports binary AUROC and F1 at the
fixed intensity threshold of 3; T3 uses Spearman correlation, QWK, and
accuracy within $\pm1$. T2 reports macro- and micro-F1; its exploratory
best-threshold values use the candidate thresholds described in Table~\ref{tab:rq2}.
Cross-domain transfer uses the same frozen parser on four public corpora.

Parser sensitivity is evaluated on the held-out split with the same dimensions,
prompt format, temperature, and output schema; the corresponding results are
reported with the main results.


\subsection{Research Questions}
The experiments examine four questions: whether the dimensions agree with the
pressure annotations, whether they provide complementary information, whether
they transfer to related forms of pressure, and whether the results are robust
to implementation choices.
\textbf{RQ1 (annotation consistency):} Do the seven dimensions align with
both the binary pressure and 0--5 intensity annotations? The evaluation reports each
dimension's Spearman correlation with these labels and its AUC for separating
coercive from non-coercive dialogues. Results are reported separately for the
disjoint held-out split ($n{=}634$) and the training split
($n{=}3{,}432$) in Sections~\ref{sec:firstrelease}--\ref{sec:fullscale}.

\textbf{RQ2 (dimension structure):} Do the seven dimensions carry distinct,
complementary signal? Report per-dimension scores and AUCs, examine the
inter-dimension correlation matrix for expected couplings (e.g., $D_3$--$D_4$)
and unexpected ones (e.g., $D_5$--$D_6$), and analyze per-family profiles
(Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\textbf{RQ3 (zero-shot generalization):} Can the profile detect pressure in
strategies that are absent from the training data? We apply the frozen
pipeline to external corpora and evaluate leave-one-type-out transfer within
the LINGUAFORCE taxonomy. The results are reported in the main results and
cross-domain sections.

\textbf{RQ4 (implementation choices):} How do aggregation and prompt design
affect the pipeline? We compare global-only, turn-mean, and combined
aggregation with the same linear readout, and evaluate zero-shot,
chain-of-thought, and self-reflection prompts. The results are reported in
the main results and Tables~\ref{tab:agg}--\ref{tab:prompt}.

\subsection{Cross-Domain Evaluation}
The cross-domain test asks whether the profile transfers to related
listener-directed pressure while remaining distinct from toxicity without a
behavioral target. The zero-shot pipeline is applied to MentalManip and
MultiManip (psychological manipulation), TalkDown (patronizing language), and
ToxicChat (toxicity). Each corpus is evaluated with its standard metric and
threshold-free AUROC.

\subsection{Dimension Contribution and Ablation}
This analysis asks whether the seven dimensions provide complementary
information. Three checks are used. First, each dimension is compared with
the gold pressure and intensity labels. Second, a simple linear model uses
the seven scores to predict the labels. Third, each dimension is removed in
turn to measure its marginal contribution.

The linear model, trained on the training split and evaluated on the
held-out split, reaches binary AUC 0.855 and intensity Spearman 0.674. These
results are close to, and slightly higher than, the profile-based LLM
pipeline (AUC 0.831, Spearman 0.665). Both systems use the seven scores
produced by the parser; the comparison therefore shows that a simple
downstream model can recover the main predictive signal from the profile. It
does not show that the linear model replaces the upstream LLM. The
leave-one-dimension-out results in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} show different
roles for different dimensions.
Removing toxicity ($D_6$) hurts pressure detection most (AUC drop 0.008),
while removing normative pressure ($D_3$) hurts intensity prediction most
(Spearman drop 0.021). Although $D_6$ has the weakest individual correlation,
it helps separate hostile dialogues that do not contain a behavioral target.
$D_4$ overlaps substantially with $D_3$; removing $D_4$ slightly improves
both metrics, which suggests redundancy or noise in this readout. The
remaining dimensions have small marginal effects. These changes should be
read as marginal effects, not as evidence that any single dimension is
indispensable. Overall, the results support a combined representation rather
than reliance on one decisive dimension.

\subsection{Main Results}
\label{sec:results}
Results are reported for pressure detection and intensity prediction,
cross-domain transfer, strategy recognition, and implementation choices.

\textbf{Pressure detection and intensity.}
Table~\ref{tab:results-t1} and Table~\ref{tab:results-t23} report the
dimension-conditioned pipeline on the 3{,}432-dialogue training split and
the disjoint 634-dialogue held-out split. The pipeline first maps each dialogue
to a seven-dimension profile (Section~\ref{sec:exp}) and then reads out the
binary pressure label and the 0--5 intensity. On the training split, it
reaches a binary AUC of 0.852 and an F1 of 0.817 at threshold 3; the
corresponding held-out values are 0.831 and 0.795. Intensity prediction reaches Spearman
$\rho=0.651$, QWK 0.595, and accuracy within $\pm1$ of 0.692 on the training
split, compared with $\rho=0.665$ and QWK 0.623 on the held-out split.
These values are similar across the two splits. On the held-out split, a direct
judge that skips the profile reaches AUC 0.780 and F1 0.755 at threshold 3; this
comparison does not isolate the contribution of individual pipeline steps.
The held-out parser comparison gives AUC 0.831 for DeepSeek and 0.838 for
GPT-6 Astra, with intensity Spearman 0.665 and 0.629, respectively. Binary
detection is similar, while intensity ranking differs between parsers.

\textbf{Cross-domain transfer.}
The cross-domain results in Table~\ref{tab:results-x} evaluate the frozen
pipeline on four external corpora. Threshold-free AUROC is 0.742 on
MentalManip, 0.706 on MultiManip, and 0.729 on TalkDown, indicating moderate
transfer to psychological manipulation and patronizing language. Transfer to
ToxicChat is weaker (AUROC 0.587), consistent with generic toxicity being
less tied to a listener-directed behavioral target. At the fixed source
threshold of 3, F1 is much lower on TalkDown (0.058), so ranking performance
does not imply that the same decision threshold transfers. The table therefore
reports AUROC as the primary cross-domain metric and fixed-threshold scores
only as descriptive reference values.

\textbf{Strategy recognition.}
Table~\ref{tab:rq2} compares a one-vs-rest logistic readout over the
seven dimensions with a supervised RoBERTa-base classifier. Training on the
3{,}432-dialogue training split and evaluating on the held-out split gives the
dimension readout family-level macro/micro-F1 of 0.772/0.827 and type-level
macro/micro-F1 of 0.480/0.668. Five-fold cross-validation on the training
release gives similar values (family 0.752/0.820; type 0.477/0.669). RoBERTa
scores 0.780/0.820 at the family level and 0.544/0.731 at the type level
on the held-out split. The dimension readout is competitive at the family
level, while RoBERTa performs better on individual types. Three types have
fewer than 30 training examples: rational persuasion ($A_3$), authority
($C_4$), and deception/gaslighting ($D_2$); their scarcity warrants caution
when interpreting type-level macro-F1. The reported F1 thresholds were
selected on the respective evaluation sets, so these are exploratory best-
threshold scores rather than performance at a prespecified threshold.

The leave-one-type-out analysis yields mean novelty AUROC 0.54, close to
chance. Separately, predicted intensity distinguishes dialogues with any
annotated strategy from those with none (AUROC 0.963). This latter test
includes known strategies and does not measure detection of held-out types
on its own.

\textbf{Implementation choices.}
The implementation ablations in Tables~\ref{tab:agg}--\ref{tab:prompt}
show that turn-only mean pooling is near chance (AUC 0.525), whereas
combining turn-level and dialogue-level profiles gives the best result (AUC
0.859, Spearman 0.679, QWK 0.680). The three prompt variants yield similar
results (AUC 0.83--0.84), indicating limited sensitivity to prompt wording.

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
The evidence has four boundaries. First, the seven dimensions are a measurement
design rather than a complete theory of influence, and the 150-dialogue
agreement study is a limited reliability check. Second, the release contains
English dialogues from sampled everyday scenarios; transfer results on four
external corpora do not establish cross-cultural or cross-lingual validity.
Third, parser comparisons cover only two models, and F1 values whose thresholds
are selected on the evaluation set are exploratory rather than fixed-threshold
estimates. Finally, the labels measure pressure expressed in dialogue, not
whether a listener complied or was harmed.


\subsection{Discussion}
The experiments support a practical interpretation of the framework. A single
profile makes heterogeneous negative strategies comparable, while the
cross-domain results suggest that some of their signal transfers beyond the
training taxonomy. The profile also makes predictions inspectable through
dimension-level evidence. These findings support measurement and analysis;
they do not imply that the dimensions are independent psychological traits or
that the benchmark predicts individual responses.


\section{Conclusion}
This paper presents LINGUAFORCE, a dataset and benchmark for measuring negative
agentive force in multi-turn dialogue. The release provides intensity labels,
seven dimension profiles, strategy labels, and an evaluation protocol. The
experiments show that the profiles support pressure detection, intensity
ordering, and transfer to related manipulation and patronizing-language
corpora. The benchmark is a measurement and analysis resource, not a causal
model of listener behavior. Future work should broaden the annotation study,
evaluate more parsers, and test other languages and forms of influence.

\appendices
\section{Ethics and Privacy}
\label{app:ethics}
The dialogues are de-identified English text. The release adds no personal
information or new data collection. The study
does not collect behavioral or demographic data; the manual checks use
already-anonymized text. Annotators may skip distressing content and take
breaks. Because the taxonomy could be misused to craft manipulative language,
the release is intended for research on detection and analysis, not automated
